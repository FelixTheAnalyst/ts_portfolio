Abstract

The implementation of South Africa’s Two-Pot retirement system on 1 September 2024 fundamentally altered household liquidity by permitting members to execute annual withdrawals from their savings component, with the tax year resetting every first of March. This study models weekly withdrawal claim dynamics using a negative-binomial regression framework incorporating an exposure offset, a decaying launch effect, an annual reset surge with exponential decay, and specific calendar effects.

To ensure complete methodological control and transparency, the estimator is custom-coded using iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS), with dispersion parameters estimated via maximum likelihood and decay rates profiled across a grid, and is validated against established benchmarks such as MASS::glm.nb. Because no official high-frequency weekly claims series is publicly available, withdrawal claims are simulated and meticulously calibrated against published South African Revenue Service (SARS) figures, enabling rigorous validation of whether the methodology accurately recovers planted effects and revealing how severely a standard Poisson model misjudges statistical uncertainty. Furthermore, empirical validation is extended to real-world search behaviour by fitting the exact model structure to Google Trends search interest data for the term “two pot” to examine the empirical robustness of the reset effect. The comprehensive tabulation of all analytical numbers and results is compiled in Section 4.9.

1 Introduction and hypotheses

The Two-Pot retirement framework permits members to execute a single withdrawal per tax year from their accumulated savings component. This regulatory structure establishes a clear behavioral mechanism: aggregate demand surges immediately when an allowance becomes available, decays gradually as members exhaust their immediate liquidity, and experiences a renewed surge when the tax year resets every first of March. These structural expectations translate into three formal hypotheses regarding the weekly claim count y_t (McCullagh and Nelder 1989; Hilbe 2011):

  • H1 (reset). Claims experience an upward surge at each annual reset, implying \beta_R > 0.
  • H2 (decay). Both the initial launch surge and subsequent annual reset surges decay over time at positive rates \lambda_L, \lambda_R > 0.
  • H3 (dispersion). Claim counts exhibit variance substantially exceeding standard Poisson assumptions, necessitating a negative-binomial formulation with finite dispersion \theta.

3 Methods

3.1 The model

For a given week t with exposure E_t (representing eligible members in millions), the claim count is modeled as:

y_t\sim\mathrm{NB}(\mu_t,\theta),\qquad \log\mu_t=\log E_t+\beta_0+\beta_L e^{-\lambda_L s_t}+\beta_R e^{-\lambda_R w_t}+\beta_M\,\text{month-end}_t+\beta_D\,\text{December}_t,

where s_t denotes weeks elapsed since launch and w_t represents weeks elapsed since the most recent annual reset week (with the reset term set to zero prior to the first reset). Variance is given by \mu + \mu^2/\theta, ensuring that the standard Poisson specification emerges as the limiting case when \theta \to \infty. Model coefficients reflect proportional percentage shifts: e^{\beta_R} - 1 measures the peak reset surge during the reset week, while \ln 2 / \lambda expresses decay rates in terms of half-life weeks. The exposure offset \log E_t is fixed with a unitary coefficient, making \beta_0 interpretable as a baseline rate per million eligible members.

3.2 Estimation from first principles

For fixed dispersion \theta and decay rates, the log link permits Newton optimization implemented via iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) (Nelder and Wedderburn 1972; Green 1984):

z_t=\eta_t-\log E_t+\frac{y_t-\mu_t}{\mu_t},\qquad w_t=\frac{\mu_t}{1+\mu_t/\theta},\qquad \hat\beta=(X^\top WX)^{-1}X^\top Wz.

Dispersion parameter \theta is updated via one-dimensional maximum likelihood conditional on fitted means \mu, iterating until log-likelihood convergence is achieved. Because decay rates enter non-linearly, they are profiled across a grid of candidate pairs (\lambda_L, \lambda_R), retaining the specification that maximizes overall log-likelihood. Standard errors treat selected decay rates as fixed, a constraint explored further in the critical reflection. For the Google Trends index, which represents a continuous index rather than discrete counts, estimation utilizes a quasi-Poisson framework scaling Poisson standard errors by the dispersion factor \hat\phi = \sum(y - \hat\mu)^2 / \hat\mu / (n - p).

3.3 Choices fixed beforehand

Decay parameter search grids span 0.02 to 0.12 (launch) and 0.10 to 0.60 (reset) weekly rates, alongside 95% Wald confidence intervals, 100 simulation recovery replications, and a fixed random seed (set.seed(2026)).

4 Results

4.1 The estimator, hand-coded and validated

# Design matrix: intercept, launch decay, reset decay, month-end and December indicators
design <- function(dates, lamL = NA, lamR = NA, useL = TRUE, useR = TRUE, useC = TRUE) {
  s <- as.numeric(dates - min(dates)) / 7                              # weeks since the first week
  w <- map_dbl(seq_along(dates), function(i) {                         # weeks since latest reset week
    p <- reset_wk[reset_wk <= dates[i]]
    if (length(p) == 0) NA_real_ else as.numeric(dates[i] - max(p)) / 7
  })
  X <- cbind(`(Intercept)` = rep(1, length(dates)))
  if (useL) X <- cbind(X, launch = exp(-lamL * s))
  if (useR) X <- cbind(X, reset = ifelse(is.na(w), 0, exp(-lamR * w)))
  if (useC) X <- cbind(X, month_end = as.numeric(month(dates) != month(dates + 6)),   # week crosses a month end
                          december  = as.numeric(month(dates) == 12))
  X
}

# Log-likelihood: Poisson (theta = Inf; quasi form for non-integer data) or negative binomial
ll_fun <- function(y, mu, theta) {
  if (is.infinite(theta)) {
    if (all(y == round(y))) sum(dpois(y, mu, log = TRUE)) else sum(y * log(mu) - mu)
  } else sum(dnbinom(y, size = theta, mu = mu, log = TRUE))
}

# IRLS for a log-link GLM with offset; theta = Inf gives Poisson
irls <- function(X, y, off, theta = Inf, tol = 1e-10, maxit = 100) {
  mu <- y + 0.1; eta <- log(mu); ll_old <- -Inf                        # safe starting values
  for (it in seq_len(maxit)) {
    wt   <- if (is.infinite(theta)) mu else mu / (1 + mu / theta)      # working weights
    z    <- eta - off + (y - mu) / mu                                  # working response
    beta <- solve(crossprod(X, wt * X), crossprod(X, wt * z))          # weighted least squares step
    eta  <- off + drop(X %*% beta); mu <- exp(eta)
    ll   <- ll_fun(y, mu, theta)
    if (abs(ll - ll_old) < tol * (abs(ll) + tol)) break                # converged
    ll_old <- ll
  }
  wt <- if (is.infinite(theta)) mu else mu / (1 + mu / theta)
  list(beta = drop(beta), mu = mu, vcov = solve(crossprod(X, wt * X)), loglik = ll, theta = theta)
}

# Negative binomial: alternate IRLS for beta and 1-D maximum likelihood for theta
fit_nb <- function(X, y, off, tol = 1e-8) {
  theta <- 10; ll_old <- -Inf
  for (k in 1:50) {
    f   <- irls(X, y, off, theta)
    opt <- optimize(function(lt) -sum(dnbinom(y, size = exp(lt), mu = f$mu, log = TRUE)),
                    interval = c(-3, 14))                              # search over log(theta)
    theta <- exp(opt$minimum); ll <- -opt$objective
    if (abs(ll - ll_old) < tol) break
    ll_old <- ll
  }
  irls(X, y, off, theta)
}

# Fit one model, profiling the decay rates over a grid
fit_model <- function(y, dates, off, family = c("nb", "pois"), useL = TRUE, useR = TRUE, useC = TRUE,
                      gL = seq(0.02, 0.12, by = 0.01), gR = seq(0.10, 0.60, by = 0.02)) {
  family <- match.arg(family)
  grid <- expand.grid(lamL = if (useL) gL else NA_real_, lamR = if (useR) gR else NA_real_)
  fits <- lapply(seq_len(nrow(grid)), function(i) {
    X <- design(dates, grid$lamL[i], grid$lamR[i], useL, useR, useC)
    f <- if (family == "nb") fit_nb(X, y, off) else irls(X, y, off)
    f$X <- X; f
  })
  grid$ll <- vapply(fits, function(f) f$loglik, numeric(1))
  b <- which.max(grid$ll)
  best <- fits[[b]]
  best$lamL <- grid$lamL[b]; best$lamR <- grid$lamR[b]; best$grid <- grid; best$family <- family
  best$k   <- ncol(best$X) + (family == "nb") + useL + useR            # coefficients + theta + decay rates
  best$aic <- -2 * best$loglik + 2 * best$k
  best
}

# Coefficient table with Wald intervals (phi scales the variance for quasi-Poisson)
coef_tab <- function(f, phi = 1) {
  se <- sqrt(diag(f$vcov) * phi)
  tibble(term = colnames(f$X), est = f$beta, se = se, lo = est - 1.96 * se, hi = est + 1.96 * se)
}

5 Discussion

The simulation and empirical findings confirm that custom-coded negative-binomial IRLS estimation successfully recovers planted behavioral effects while demonstrating why standard Poisson GLMs fail completely in the presence of massive overdispersion. While the Google Trends analysis provides real-world validation of public search attention dynamics around regulatory resets, search interest measures public curiosity rather than actual financial withdrawals. Furthermore, relying on simulated claims data represents a fundamental limitation, as calibration relies on sparse official anchors. Finally, the exponential decay formulation serves as a reduced-form proxy for member pool exhaustion under the rule permitting one annual withdrawal per tax year.

What it does not say. The simulation recovers what I put in, so it validates the method, not the behavioural story. Nothing here shows what actual withdrawal counts did. The Trends index measures attention, not claims.

6 Critical reflection

Several methodological boundaries merit explicit recognition. First, utilizing simulated claims means parameter recovery demonstrates internal consistency under the true data-generating process rather than proving real-world behavioral dynamics. Second, profiling decay rates and treating them as fixed when calculating standard errors leads to slight coverage understatement, a trade-off accepted for computational tractability. Third, standard GLM inference assumes temporal independence, whereas real-world claims and search series exhibit serial correlation. Incorporating dynamic state-space structures, as explored in Project 4, provides a natural pathway to resolve these limitations.

# Abstract {.unnumbered}

The implementation of South Africa's Two-Pot retirement system on 1 September 2024 fundamentally altered household liquidity by permitting members to execute annual withdrawals from their savings component, with the tax year resetting every first of March. This study models weekly withdrawal claim dynamics using a negative-binomial regression framework incorporating an exposure offset, a decaying launch effect, an annual reset surge with exponential decay, and specific calendar effects. 

\vspace{0.2cm}

To ensure complete methodological control and transparency, the estimator is custom-coded using iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS), with dispersion parameters estimated via maximum likelihood and decay rates profiled across a grid, and is validated against established benchmarks such as `MASS::glm.nb`. Because no official high-frequency weekly claims series is publicly available, withdrawal claims are simulated and meticulously calibrated against published South African Revenue Service (SARS) figures, enabling rigorous validation of whether the methodology accurately recovers planted effects and revealing how severely a standard Poisson model misjudges statistical uncertainty. Furthermore, empirical validation is extended to real-world search behaviour by fitting the exact model structure to Google Trends search interest data for the term "two pot" to examine the empirical robustness of the reset effect. The comprehensive tabulation of all analytical numbers and results is compiled in @sec-evidence.

# Introduction and hypotheses {#sec-intro}

The Two-Pot retirement framework permits members to execute a single withdrawal per tax year from their accumulated savings component. This regulatory structure establishes a clear behavioral mechanism: aggregate demand surges immediately when an allowance becomes available, decays gradually as members exhaust their immediate liquidity, and experiences a renewed surge when the tax year resets every first of March. These structural expectations translate into three formal hypotheses regarding the weekly claim count $y_t$ [@mccullagh1989; @hilbe2011]:

- **H1 (reset).** Claims experience an upward surge at each annual reset, implying $\beta_R > 0$.
- **H2 (decay).** Both the initial launch surge and subsequent annual reset surges decay over time at positive rates $\lambda_L, \lambda_R > 0$.
- **H3 (dispersion).**  Claim counts exhibit variance substantially exceeding standard Poisson assumptions, necessitating a negative-binomial formulation with finite dispersion $\theta$.

# Methods {#sec-methods}

## The model

For a given week $t$ with exposure $E_t$ (representing eligible members in millions), the claim count is modeled as:

$$y_t\sim\mathrm{NB}(\mu_t,\theta),\qquad \log\mu_t=\log E_t+\beta_0+\beta_L e^{-\lambda_L s_t}+\beta_R e^{-\lambda_R w_t}+\beta_M\,\text{month-end}_t+\beta_D\,\text{December}_t,$$

where $s_t$ denotes weeks elapsed since launch and $w_t$ represents weeks elapsed since the most recent annual reset week (with the reset term set to zero prior to the first reset). Variance is given by $\mu + \mu^2/\theta$, ensuring that the standard Poisson specification emerges as the limiting case when $\theta \to \infty$. Model coefficients reflect proportional percentage shifts: $e^{\beta_R} - 1$ measures the peak reset surge during the reset week, while $\ln 2 / \lambda$ expresses decay rates in terms of half-life weeks. The exposure offset $\log E_t$ is fixed with a unitary coefficient, making $\beta_0$ interpretable as a baseline rate per million eligible members.

## Estimation from first principles

For fixed dispersion $\theta$ and decay rates, the log link permits Newton optimization implemented via iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) [@nelder1972; @green1984]:

$$z_t=\eta_t-\log E_t+\frac{y_t-\mu_t}{\mu_t},\qquad w_t=\frac{\mu_t}{1+\mu_t/\theta},\qquad \hat\beta=(X^\top WX)^{-1}X^\top Wz.$$

Dispersion parameter $\theta$ is updated via one-dimensional maximum likelihood conditional on fitted means $\mu$, iterating until log-likelihood convergence is achieved. Because decay rates enter non-linearly, they are profiled across a grid of candidate pairs $(\lambda_L, \lambda_R)$, retaining the specification that maximizes overall log-likelihood. Standard errors treat selected decay rates as fixed, a constraint explored further in the critical reflection. For the Google Trends index, which represents a continuous index rather than discrete counts, estimation utilizes a quasi-Poisson framework scaling Poisson standard errors by the dispersion factor $\hat\phi = \sum(y - \hat\mu)^2 / \hat\mu / (n - p)$.

## Choices fixed beforehand

Decay parameter search grids span $0.02$ to $0.12$ (launch) and $0.10$ to $0.60$ (reset) weekly rates, alongside 95% Wald confidence intervals, 100 simulation recovery replications, and a fixed random seed `(set.seed(2026))`.

# Design matrix: intercept, launch decay, reset decay, month-end and December indicators
design <- function(dates, lamL = NA, lamR = NA, useL = TRUE, useR = TRUE, useC = TRUE) {
  s <- as.numeric(dates - min(dates)) / 7                              # weeks since the first week
  w <- map_dbl(seq_along(dates), function(i) {                         # weeks since latest reset week
    p <- reset_wk[reset_wk <= dates[i]]
    if (length(p) == 0) NA_real_ else as.numeric(dates[i] - max(p)) / 7
  })
  X <- cbind(`(Intercept)` = rep(1, length(dates)))
  if (useL) X <- cbind(X, launch = exp(-lamL * s))
  if (useR) X <- cbind(X, reset = ifelse(is.na(w), 0, exp(-lamR * w)))
  if (useC) X <- cbind(X, month_end = as.numeric(month(dates) != month(dates + 6)),   # week crosses a month end
                          december  = as.numeric(month(dates) == 12))
  X
}

# Log-likelihood: Poisson (theta = Inf; quasi form for non-integer data) or negative binomial
ll_fun <- function(y, mu, theta) {
  if (is.infinite(theta)) {
    if (all(y == round(y))) sum(dpois(y, mu, log = TRUE)) else sum(y * log(mu) - mu)
  } else sum(dnbinom(y, size = theta, mu = mu, log = TRUE))
}

# IRLS for a log-link GLM with offset; theta = Inf gives Poisson
irls <- function(X, y, off, theta = Inf, tol = 1e-10, maxit = 100) {
  mu <- y + 0.1; eta <- log(mu); ll_old <- -Inf                        # safe starting values
  for (it in seq_len(maxit)) {
    wt   <- if (is.infinite(theta)) mu else mu / (1 + mu / theta)      # working weights
    z    <- eta - off + (y - mu) / mu                                  # working response
    beta <- solve(crossprod(X, wt * X), crossprod(X, wt * z))          # weighted least squares step
    eta  <- off + drop(X %*% beta); mu <- exp(eta)
    ll   <- ll_fun(y, mu, theta)
    if (abs(ll - ll_old) < tol * (abs(ll) + tol)) break                # converged
    ll_old <- ll
  }
  wt <- if (is.infinite(theta)) mu else mu / (1 + mu / theta)
  list(beta = drop(beta), mu = mu, vcov = solve(crossprod(X, wt * X)), loglik = ll, theta = theta)
}

# Negative binomial: alternate IRLS for beta and 1-D maximum likelihood for theta
fit_nb <- function(X, y, off, tol = 1e-8) {
  theta <- 10; ll_old <- -Inf
  for (k in 1:50) {
    f   <- irls(X, y, off, theta)
    opt <- optimize(function(lt) -sum(dnbinom(y, size = exp(lt), mu = f$mu, log = TRUE)),
                    interval = c(-3, 14))                              # search over log(theta)
    theta <- exp(opt$minimum); ll <- -opt$objective
    if (abs(ll - ll_old) < tol) break
    ll_old <- ll
  }
  irls(X, y, off, theta)
}

# Fit one model, profiling the decay rates over a grid
fit_model <- function(y, dates, off, family = c("nb", "pois"), useL = TRUE, useR = TRUE, useC = TRUE,
                      gL = seq(0.02, 0.12, by = 0.01), gR = seq(0.10, 0.60, by = 0.02)) {
  family <- match.arg(family)
  grid <- expand.grid(lamL = if (useL) gL else NA_real_, lamR = if (useR) gR else NA_real_)
  fits <- lapply(seq_len(nrow(grid)), function(i) {
    X <- design(dates, grid$lamL[i], grid$lamR[i], useL, useR, useC)
    f <- if (family == "nb") fit_nb(X, y, off) else irls(X, y, off)
    f$X <- X; f
  })
  grid$ll <- vapply(fits, function(f) f$loglik, numeric(1))
  b <- which.max(grid$ll)
  best <- fits[[b]]
  best$lamL <- grid$lamL[b]; best$lamR <- grid$lamR[b]; best$grid <- grid; best$family <- family
  best$k   <- ncol(best$X) + (family == "nb") + useL + useR            # coefficients + theta + decay rates
  best$aic <- -2 * best$loglik + 2 * best$k
  best
}

# Coefficient table with Wald intervals (phi scales the variance for quasi-Poisson)
coef_tab <- function(f, phi = 1) {
  se <- sqrt(diag(f$vcov) * phi)
  tibble(term = colnames(f$X), est = f$beta, se = se, lo = est - 1.96 * se, hi = est + 1.96 * se)
}

## Does inference hold up? A recovery study {#sec-recovery}

```{r}
#| label: recovery
#| tbl-cap: "Over 100 simulated series: bias and spread of each estimate, and how often the 95% Wald interval covers the truth under the negative binomial model and under Poisson."

R_rep <- 100
gL_c <- seq(0.02, 0.12, by = 0.02); gR_c <- seq(0.10, 0.60, by = 0.10)   # coarser grids keep this quick
set.seed(2026)
rec <- map_dfr(seq_len(R_rep), function(r) {
  yr <- rnbinom(n_wk, size = tru$theta, mu = mu_true)                  # fresh series from the same truth
  f  <- fit_model(yr, wk, off, "nb", gL = gL_c, gR = gR_c)
  p  <- irls(f$X, yr, off)                                              # Poisson at the same decay rates
  se_nb <- sqrt(diag(f$vcov)); se_po <- sqrt(diag(p$vcov))
  tibble(term = colnames(f$X), truth = truth_b, est = f$beta,
         cov_nb = abs(f$beta - truth_b) < 1.96 * se_nb,
         cov_po = abs(p$beta - truth_b) < 1.96 * se_po)
})
rec_tab <- rec |> group_by(term) |>
  summarise(Bias = mean(est - truth), SD = sd(est), `NB coverage` = mean(cov_nb),
            `Poisson coverage` = mean(cov_po), .groups = "drop")
rec_tab |> kable(digits = 3)
cov_nb_R <- rec_tab$`NB coverage`[rec_tab$term == "reset"]
cov_po_R <- rec_tab$`Poisson coverage`[rec_tab$term == "reset"]
```

Across `r R_rep` simulated replications, parameter estimates remain unbiased. For the key reset effect, negative-binomial confidence intervals achieve coverage in `r sprintf("%.0f%%", 100 * cov_nb_R)` of trials, compared to `r sprintf("%.0f%%", 100 * cov_po_R)` for Poisson, whose severe underdispersion leads to drastically suppressed standard errors and invalid inference.

The Ljung-Box test on the residuals gives $p$ = `r sprintf("%.3f", lb_p)`. # Discussion {#sec-discussion}

The simulation and empirical findings confirm that custom-coded negative-binomial IRLS estimation successfully recovers planted behavioral effects while demonstrating why standard Poisson GLMs fail completely in the presence of massive overdispersion. While the Google Trends analysis provides real-world validation of public search attention dynamics around regulatory resets, search interest measures public curiosity rather than actual financial withdrawals. Furthermore, relying on simulated claims data represents a fundamental limitation, as calibration relies on sparse official anchors. Finally, the exponential decay formulation serves as a reduced-form proxy for member pool exhaustion under the rule permitting one annual withdrawal per tax year.

**What it does not say.** The simulation recovers what I put in, so it validates the method, not the behavioural story. Nothing here shows what actual withdrawal counts did. The Trends index measures attention, not claims.

# Critical reflection {#sec-reflection}

Several methodological boundaries merit explicit recognition. First, utilizing simulated claims means parameter recovery demonstrates internal consistency under the true data-generating process rather than proving real-world behavioral dynamics. Second, profiling decay rates and treating them as fixed when calculating standard errors leads to slight coverage understatement, a trade-off accepted for computational tractability. Third, standard GLM inference assumes temporal independence, whereas real-world claims and search series exhibit serial correlation. Incorporating dynamic state-space structures, as explored in Project 4, provides a natural pathway to resolve these limitations.
